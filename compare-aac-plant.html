A complete comparison

Twelve factors.
One accountable decision.

Each factor connects a technical choice to commercial risk, operational stability or future flexibility.

01

Production basis

Mould volume, batches, operating hours, density, product mix and saleable output.

02

Equipment scope

Machinery, auxiliaries, controls, handling systems and battery limits.

03

Automation boundary

Where manual intervention remains in normal operation and fault recovery.

Power, steam and water assumptions on the same production basis.

What the supplier designs, manufactures, assembles and tests.

Comparable references supported by operating records—not observation alone.

Service and spares

Expansion readiness

Future capacity, panel integration and layout constraints.

Evaluate

Turn every promise
into evidence.

A supplier presentation can establish interest. It cannot replace measurable assumptions, physical verification or contractual clarity.

01

Normalize the proposal

Place each offer into one scope-responsibility matrix. Record supply, erection, cabling, piping, integration, commissioning and testing for every plant section.

02

Verify how capacity is calculated

Request mould volume, batches per hour, operating hours, pre-curing constraints, cutting cycle, autoclave loading, curing cycle and saleable-output assumptions.

03

Map automation by function

Identify where operators initiate movement, confirm sequences, recover faults, record quality data or manually handle material. Compare functions—not labels.

04

Use the same utility basis

Separate connected load from consumption. Compare power, steam, water and manpower for the same production, product mix and shift pattern.

Comparison rule

If two capacity or utility figures use different assumptions, they are not yet comparable.

Physical verification

Visit the places
where claims become visible.

01 / Workshop

Inspect manufacturing capability

A workshop visit should be an evidence review—not a visual judgement of size or housekeeping alone.

  • In-house and bought-out equipment scope
  • Fabrication, machining and assembly capability
  • Material traceability and inspection stages
  • Current workload and project resources
  • Testing and pre-dispatch practices

Do not infer financial strength from workshop appearance. That requires separate due diligence.

02 / Running plant

Verify operating performance

Select references close to the proposed capacity, raw-material route, automation level, age and product mix.

  • Recorded production and saleable output
  • Manpower and manual interventions
  • Product quality and downtime records
  • Utility history where available
  • Service and spare-parts experience

Observe the plant, speak with the owner and operating team, then cross-check recollections with records.

Decide

Choose the solution
whose responsibilities are clear.

The objective is not to find the longest proposal. It is to find the configuration that fits your project and can be verified.

Decision area Question to answer
Technical fit Does the configuration suit the required products, raw materials and output?
Evidence Which important claims are supported by documents or relevant operating plants?
Scope completeness Are all equipment, services, interfaces and exclusions visible?
Execution capability Are design, manufacturing, project and commissioning resources demonstrated?
Lifecycle responsibility Are service, spares, training and documentation adequate?
Contractual clarity Are performance obligations and acceptance conditions measurable?
Future readiness Can the layout support realistic expansion or panel requirements?
Do not let a high total score hide a critical failure.

Unsupported capacity, incomplete safety responsibility, unclear acceptance terms or an essential missing plant section should be treated as decision gates—not merely low-scoring items.

Before you finalize

Eight red flags
worth stopping for.

  1. 01Capacity without cycle, operating-hour or saleable-output assumptions.
  2. 02‘Fully automatic’ without a process-level automation boundary.
  3. 03Utility or manpower savings without a comparable baseline.
  4. 04Project references without the supplier’s exact scope.
  5. 05Buyer-scope items without clear interface responsibility.
  6. 06Commissioning dependent on unnamed resources.
  7. 07No measurable performance test or acceptance protocol.
  8. 08Limited access to relevant customers or technical personnel.

Investor questions

Clear answers.
Before commitment.

No. Price should be compared only after scope, assumptions, performance obligations and lifecycle responsibilities are normalized. A lower price may be commercially sound when the proposal remains technically complete and verifiable.

One closely comparable reference provides useful evidence. Additional references improve confidence when technology, capacity, raw materials, product mix or plant age differ.

Map the complete process and record every routine action, manual transfer, operator confirmation, fault-recovery task and quality-data entry. Compare functions rather than labels.

Ask which assumptions convert mould volume and production cycles into saleable cubic metres per day. Without those assumptions, headline capacities are not directly comparable.

Clear accountability is more important than a particular contracting model. Every interface needs a competent, named and contractually responsible party.

A plant reasonably close to the proposed technology, capacity, raw-material route, product mix and automation level—and where operating records can be reviewed.
