DIRECT ANSWER

What goes into
an AAC block?

AAC is made from a silica source—normally fly ash or finely ground quartz sand—combined with quicklime, OPC cement, gypsum, aluminium powder or paste, process water and carefully controlled additives.

There is no universal recipe. Chemistry, fineness, moisture, reactivity, target density and autoclaving conditions determine the workable proportion.

01 · MATERIAL INTELLIGENCE

Each input has one
critical job.

Select a material to review its chemical limits, physical behaviour and role in the AAC reaction system.

PRIMARY SILICA DONOR

Class F fly ash

Fly ash is the preferred silica source across many Indian AAC plants because amorphous silica reacts efficiently in the alkaline slurry. Consistency matters as much as headline chemistry.

Combined SiO₂ + Al₂O₃ + Fe₂O₃ Minimum 70%
Reactive silica Approximately 45–50%
Calcium oxide Below 10% for Class F
Loss on ignition Below 5%; preferably below 3%
Fineness <15% residue on 45 µm sieve; Blaine 2,800–3,800 cm²/g
Reference standard IS 3812 (Part 1), verify current applicable edition
High unburnt carbon increases water demand and can disturb aluminium-driven pore formation, cake stability and final strength.
ALTERNATIVE SILICA DONOR

Finely ground quartz sand

Used where fly ash is unavailable or for premium pure-white AAC blocks. Wet ball-milling to high fineness is mandatory to ensure hydrothermal reaction in the autoclave.

SiO₂ content Minimum 85–90%
Fe₂O₃ content < 1.5% (for white block grade)
Fineness after wet milling Residue on 45 µm sieve < 12–15%
Clay / silt content < 3%
Reference standard IS 1911 / ASTM C144
Sand slurry requires dedicated wet ball mills with continuous classification to guarantee uniform silica dissolution during autoclaving.
PRIMARY CALCIUM OXIDE & EXOTHERMIC AGENT

High-reactivity quicklime (CaO)

Quicklime drives the hydration temperature, initiates slurry expansion with aluminium, and provides reactive Ca(OH)₂ for Tobermorite crystal synthesis.

Active Available CaO Minimum 75–85%
Reactivity (t60) Under 4–8 minutes (fast to medium reactive)
Slaking temperature rise ΔT > 40°C in standard slaking test
Residue / unburnt stone < 5%
Reference standard IS 712 / EN 459-1
Over-reactive lime causes flash setting and boiling; under-reactive lime leads to cake sinking and delayed autoclaving cycles.
BINDER & INITIAL GREEN STRENGTH

Ordinary Portland Cement (53 Grade)

Provides early green strength to permit rapid wire cutting without deformation or edge chipping while stabilizing the rising cake before autoclaving.

Grade / Type OPC 53 Grade (preferred) or OPC 43 Grade
Initial setting time Minimum 30–60 minutes
Compressive strength (28 day) Minimum 53 MPa (IS 12269)
Fineness (Blaine) Minimum 3,000 cm²/g
Reference standard IS 12269 / IS 269
PPC (Pozzolana Portland Cement) is generally avoided as the slower early hydration compromises cutting wire cycle times.
SETTING REGULATOR & EXPANSION STABILIZER

Gypsum / Anhydrite

Regulates the quicklime slaking reaction, retards the hydration of tricalcium aluminate (C3A), and accelerates hydrothermal Tobermorite crystal crystallization.

CaSO₄ · 2H₂O content Minimum 80–85%
Purity High sulphate purity, low chlorides
Fineness Ground to < 10% on 90 µm sieve
Moisture (if phosphogypsum) < 8% with free acid neutralized
Reference standard IS 1298 / ASTM C22
Inadequate gypsum causes rapid slurry setting and micro-cracking during the autoclave heating phase.
GAS-FORMING / EXPANSION AGENT

Aluminium powder / paste

Reacts with Ca(OH)₂ in alkaline slurry to generate micro-bubbles of hydrogen gas, creating the 80% pore volume that gives AAC its lightweight and thermal insulating properties.

Active metallic aluminium Minimum 88–92%
Grain shape & grading Flake-shaped particles, controlled size distribution
Hydrogen gas evolution rate 16–22 minutes at 45°C slurry temperature
Solvent / carrier Degreased water-dispersible paste or dry leafing flake
Reference standard IS 438 / EN 12390
Dosing precision within ±5 grams per batch is essential. Even minor variations directly alter final block density and compressive strength.
SURFACTANT & PORE STABILIZER

Soluble soap oil / mould release

Reduces slurry surface tension to ensure uniform hydrogen bubble dispersion and prevents bubble coalescence before the cake solidifies.

Active matter Formulated anionic/non-ionic surfactant
pH range 7.0 – 9.0
Solubility in water 100% complete dispersion
Dosage rate 0.02 – 0.05% of dry solids weight
Application Direct slurry addition & mould demoulding film
Ensures spherical, disconnected closed-cell pore structure, keeping water absorption low and thermal resistance high.

04 · REACTION SCIENCE

Strength is formed
inside the autoclave.

The recipe first creates a stable green cake. Saturated steam then converts calcium and reactive silica into a crystalline strength-giving matrix.

TARGET HYDROTHERMAL WINDOW
Ca(OH)₂ + reactive SiO₂ + H₂O →
C–S–H → 1.1 nm tobermorite

Indicative report basis: 180–200°C, approximately 1.0–1.3 MPa and a controlled CaO/SiO₂ molar balance. Final settings must follow the validated recipe and equipment design.

One continuous chemical sequence

Quicklime slaking and cement hydration establish temperature, alkalinity and early strength. Aluminium generates temporary hydrogen bubbles. During saturated-steam curing, dissolved silica combines with calcium-bearing phases to form interlocking tobermorite crystals around the pore walls.

01

Rise & set

Align gas evolution with slurry stiffening so the cake expands without collapse.

02

Cut

Achieve enough green strength for accurate wire cutting without tearing.

03

Autoclave

Use controlled saturated steam to develop strength and dimensional stability.

05 · QUALIFICATION PROTOCOL

Test before you
design the plant.

A bankable consumption estimate starts with representative samples from the actual proposed supply sources.

01

Representative sampling

Collect source-specific samples across realistic operating periods—not a single best-quality bag.

02

Chemical analysis

Measure silica, alumina, iron, calcium, magnesium, sulphates, carbon and loss on ignition as applicable.

03

Physical testing

Verify moisture, fineness, particle-size distribution, bulk density and lime slaking behaviour.

04

Trial mix

Observe slurry flow, temperature curve, rise, fallback, green strength and cutting window.

05

Autoclave validation

Confirm dry density, compressive strength, shrinkage and internal pore structure after curing.

06

Commercial benchmark

Calculate kg and delivered cost per saleable m³, including moisture correction and rejection.

ENGINEERING NOTE

The lowest material price per tonne may not produce the lowest cost per saleable cubic metre. Variable fly ash or lime can increase binder demand, cycle time, density and rejection.

06 · TECHNICAL FAQ

Practical questions,
direct answers.

Fly ash (or finely ground quartz silica sand) constitutes 55% to 65% of the total dry solid mass, making it the primary silica donor. It combines hydrothermally with calcium oxide from quicklime and OPC cement inside the autoclave at 190°C to synthesize tobermorite crystal structure.
No. Fly ash varies significantly between power stations in unburnt carbon (loss on ignition), reactive silica content, and particulate fineness. High carbon increases water demand and interferes with aluminium foaming, requiring specific recipe tuning and water-to-solid adjustments for every unique source.
Quicklime reactivity determines the rate of slaking temperature rise. A fast-to-medium reactive lime (T60 under 4–8 minutes) ensures the slurry reaches 60–65°C quickly enough to synchronize aluminium hydrogen gas evolution with initial slurry stiffening, preventing cake collapse or delayed setting.
Approximately 0.38 to 0.40 kg of active leafing aluminium powder or water-dispersible paste is required per cubic metre for standard B06 density class (550–650 kg/m³). Even minor fluctuations (±10 grams) alter block density and compressive strength significantly.
Raw material consumption should always be monitored per gross cake volume (production output) as well as per net saleable packed cubic metres. Tracking both identifies process waste, cutting wire top-crust trimming losses, and autoclave rejection rates.
