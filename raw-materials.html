Direct answer

What goes into an AAC block?

AAC is made from a silica source—normally fly ash or finely ground quartz sand—combined with quicklime, OPC cement, gypsum, aluminium powder or paste, process water and carefully controlled additives.

There is no universal recipe. Chemistry, fineness, moisture, reactivity, target density and autoclaving conditions determine the workable proportion.

55–65% Fly ash / silica source
10–18% OPC cement
15–25% Quicklime
2–5% Gypsum / anhydrite
0.05–0.10% Aluminium, of dry solids

Primary silica donor

Class F fly ash

Fly ash is the preferred silica source across many Indian AAC plants because amorphous silica reacts efficiently in the alkaline slurry. Consistency matters as much as headline chemistry.

Combined SiO₂ + Al₂O₃ + Fe₂O₃

High unburnt carbon increases water demand and can disturb aluminium-driven pore formation, cake stability and final strength.

Alternative silica donor

Quartz silica sand

Sand is suitable where consistent fly ash is unavailable, but crystalline quartz requires controlled wet grinding and sufficient hydrothermal energy.

Sand and fly ash are not one-to-one substitutes. Grinding, water demand, binder balance and autoclave settings must be revalidated.

Alkaline activator · Calcium donor

Quicklime

Quicklime supplies calcium, creates the high-pH environment and releases heat during slaking. Its reaction curve directly affects cake rise, temperature and cutting time.

Active CaO
Above 85%; preferred 88–94%
Magnesium oxide
Below approximately 2.0–2.5%
Silica impurity
Low; commonly controlled around 1.5–5%
Slaking reactivity, t₆₀
Target 4–14 minutes
Particle size
Approximately 45–75 μm / 100–300 BSS mesh
Very fast lime can cause thermal spikes and cake fallback. Slow lime can delay setting and damage the green cake during cutting.

Hydraulic binder

OPC cement

OPC provides early green strength for mould handling and wire cutting, while contributing calcium and maintaining alkalinity for gas generation.

More cement is not automatically better. Excess consumption increases cost and may alter cake temperature, setting and cutting performance.

Sulfate regulator

Gypsum or anhydrite

Gypsum regulates early hydration, moderates setting and supports formation of the desired mineral structure during autoclaving.

Calcium sulfate purity
Approximately 70–90% or better, source dependent

Gypsum dosage must be matched with lime reactivity and cement behaviour; it is a process-control input, not merely a filler.

Gas expansion agent

Metallic aluminium

Aluminium reacts in the alkaline slurry to release hydrogen. The temporary bubbles form the cellular pore structure that gives AAC its low density.

Active metallic aluminium
Approximately 85–95%
Particle morphology
Leafing, flake-like
Median particle size
Approximately 25–45 μm
Screen passage
More than 98% through 75 μm / 200 mesh
Typical dosage
0.38–0.40 kg per finished m³ for B06 benchmark mixes
Small dosing errors have a large effect on density, cake height, pore structure and rejection. Automated precision dosing is strongly recommended.

Process modifier · Release aid

Soluble soap oil

A water-dispersible emulsion can moderate early reaction, stabilise pore walls and act as a mould-release boundary lubricant.

Typical slurry dosage
Approximately 0.3–0.7 L per finished m³, mix dependent
Mould-release dilution
Commonly 1:5 to 1:20 with water
Primary functions
Bubble stability, water retention, clean demoulding
Control requirement
Compatibility trials with lime and aluminium system
Only use plant-approved formulations. A change in surfactant chemistry can shift gas-release timing and cake stability.

04 · Reaction science

Strength is formed inside the autoclave.

The recipe first creates a stable green cake. Saturated steam then converts calcium and reactive silica into a crystalline strength-giving matrix.

Target hydrothermal window

Ca(OH)₂ + reactive SiO₂ + H₂O → C–S–H → 1.1 nm tobermorite

Indicative report basis: 180–200°C, approximately 1.0–1.3 MPa and a controlled CaO/SiO₂ molar balance. Final settings must follow the validated recipe and equipment design.

One continuous chemical sequence

Quicklime slaking and cement hydration establish temperature, alkalinity and early strength. Aluminium generates temporary hydrogen bubbles. During saturated-steam curing, dissolved silica combines with calcium-bearing phases to form interlocking tobermorite crystals around the pore walls.

05 · Qualification protocol

Test before you design the plant.

A bankable consumption estimate starts with representative samples from the actual proposed supply sources.

01

Representative sampling

Collect source-specific samples across realistic operating periods—not a single best-quality bag.

02

Chemical analysis

Measure silica, alumina, iron, calcium, magnesium, sulphates, carbon and loss on ignition as applicable.

03

Physical testing

Verify moisture, fineness, particle-size distribution, bulk density and lime slaking behaviour.

04

Trial mix

Observe slurry flow, temperature curve, rise, fallback, green strength and cutting window.

05

Autoclave validation

Confirm dry density, compressive strength, shrinkage and internal pore structure after curing.

06

Commercial benchmark

Calculate kg and delivered cost per saleable m³, including moisture correction and rejection.

ENGINEERING NOTE

The lowest material price per tonne may not produce the lowest cost per saleable cubic metre. Variable fly ash or lime can increase binder demand, cycle time, density and rejection.
